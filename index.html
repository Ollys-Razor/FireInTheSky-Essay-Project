Fire in the Sky

One day, towards the end of the COVID lockdowns, I sat, hunched over my phone. I felt buried beneath the enormity of the oncoming climate disaster, a disaster built of actions not taken. I was buried under my own failures too. I sat, and hid, amongst the petty diversions of the internet. My naive hopes for the world had worn away, and I felt better burying my head in the proverbial digital sand. It was in this state that I stubbled upon it: A song; to me it was a beacon. It carried a burning hope, pride, and faith in human achievement. Called "Fire in the Sky", it was about spaceflight; to me the meaning seemed much greater than that. In it I found a spark of a better hope, one that could not be worn down by the world.

…Gagarin was the first, back in 1961, When like Icarus, undaunted, he climbed to reach the sun...

In Greek myth, Icarus fled the Labyrinth and the Minotaur, flying on wings made by his father from wax and feathers. He escaped only to fly too close to the sun, his wings melting off of him as he plummeted to the earth. I was taught Icarus's story as a cautionary tale. The message was clear: don't ask for too much; don't fly too close to the sun. Yet here the song compares Yuri Gagarin to "Icarus undaunted". An image is conjured of an Icarus who, like Gagarin on the very first crewed orbit orbital flight, knew the danger he faced. An Icarus who flew towards the sun anyway. Here the story is not one of caution, but one of triumph; a mark set in myth that was not surpassed in history until Gagarin himself. Though Icarus fell, perhaps it was a will to find the very limit of possibility that drove him, not hubris.

At the start of the covid nineteen pandemic I found myself in a downward spiral. I had spent three years in college, each semester worse than the last. Time management and organization have always been challenges for me, and there were only so many all-nighters I could pull before I reached a crisis point. Trapped as I was, alone in a quarantined dorm-room, I lost all ability to focus on my education. My schedule ceased to exist, and I felt as though I was drifting , unable to track time, yet always certain that I had forgotten something important. Within a few months, I dropped out.

I took this failure hard. I was good at learning, if not school. My mind was ever my greatest strength, amongst my many weaknesses. To fail in this way was perhaps the greatest blow to my self image I have yet suffered. If I never got my degree, I would be the first member of my family in generations to fail to get at least a bachelors degree. No one told me that I had to meet that standard, but I held myself to it nonetheless.

In my depressed state, the news of the world weighed heavily on my mind. Every failure to address the pandemic or the looming climate disaster seemed yet more evidence that we were doomed, and there was no point even to try. Yet if Icarus's failure could have meaning, then perhaps so could mine. The song conjured a feeling of purpose in failure that resonated deeply for me. It reminded me that there is purpose and value in trying, even if you cannot succeed.

…. we gave ourselves a decade to put fire on the moon, and Apollo told the world, we can do it if we try

These lyrics called up a deep seated memory in me. Even as I heard them I heard other words too, spoken by then president Kennedy:

"We choose to go to the Moon in this decade and do the other things, not because they are easy, but because they are hard"

I remember hearing those words for the first time as a small child, watching a NASA documentary on our old CRT TV with rapt attention, sandwiched between my parents on our old couch. I was a huge space nerd, and so as a child this expectation, to choose hard challenges because we could, formed the basis of my identity as an American. It inspired a naive hope, that if we once did that, we could do anything.

My childhood hope was naive, because I only felt the first part, the "we can do it", and thought little of the "if we try". Why would we not try? The world seemed simple: face the challenges of the world head on, and overcome them. My hope, in its naivety, proved fragile. As I grew up, so did my understanding of the looming climate crisis, of the injustices still faced by so many, and of the failures to address either. Most people seemed unwilling to take risks to improve the world. I heard the same refrain in many different words: "we can't do it". Each new piece of knowledge eroded my hope down to little more than a memory. By the time I found myself filing the paperwork to drop out of college little was left. My hope had narrowed to merely building a decent life for myself, and even that had collapsed. I think I felt ashamed to have ever been so hopeful; what an idiot I was!

Sitting alone and listening to this song, I felt some of that hope rekindle within me. The hope I felt then was not the naive hope that things would get better. Instead it was an active thing. This hope I felt was a choice, and an obligation to choose, again and again, that our effort mattered, that it had meaning, that we could leave the world better than we found it. My life did not magically improve that day, but the words of this song echoed in my ears then, as they still do now, a reminder of a choice I must keep making.

…Yet the Gods do not give lightly of the powers they have madeAnd with Challenger and seven, once again the price is paid…

There is much that is wrong with this line. Challenger's crew were not struck down by the gods. It was human choices, human negligence, that condemned them to fall like Icarus, wreathed in fire, to the ocean below. But for all that is wrong, there is something deeply right here too: there is a cost to taking hard fights. Failure can mean death and suffering, yet this is no reason to give up. Instead, the song paints the crew as heroes, because, like Icarus, they died trying to do something great.

Though my challenge was far the lesser, the same goes for me. I could never have dropped out of Gustavus, had I never gone. Now I am a student again, and I could yet fail twice. It is the active hope this song helped inspire that carries me forward today. The hope I once had would fail me here, because it relied on the belief that I would succeed. The hope I have now will not fail, because it is based on the belief that my attempting a challenge has meaning no matter what.

… they passed from us to glory, riding fire in the sky… now the rest is up to us, and there's a future to be won…

I listened to "Fire in the sky" many times that day, and countless times since. The song is not about spaceflight to me. Instead, it holds spaceflight up as a symbol. That symbol stands for every challenge we face, and especially the hard ones. It could be an apocalyptic threat like the climate disaster, or a choice to take a difficult course that is not required. Through this symbol, I learned how to hope again, even in the face of adversity. You can have hope, even in a long shot or a hail-mary attempt, if you believe that your trying is worth something, even if you fail.

It is hard to take on any challenge in the absence of hope, and we collectively face great challenges ahead of us. For this reason, I believe I have an obligation to hope for the future, and to align my actions with that hope as well. I believe that obligation rests on every person's shoulders as well. We can do it, whatever it may be, if we try.
